# Employee vs contractor

Whether you are an employee or an independent contractor changes how you are taxed, who handles the paperwork, and what you are responsible for. The classification is not a matter of preference or what a contract calls you; it follows from the nature of the working relationship, and getting it wrong has consequences for both sides.

## What the distinction turns on

The core question is how much control the payer has over the work. An employee is generally told not just what to do but how, when, and where to do it, uses the payer's tools, works set hours, and is integrated into the business. An independent contractor controls how the work gets done, often serves multiple clients, supplies their own tools, and is in business for themselves. United States authorities look at the overall relationship across several factors rather than any single one, weighing behavioral control, financial control, and the nature of the relationship together.

## Why it matters for tax

For an employee, the employer withholds income tax from each paycheck, pays half of the Social Security and Medicare taxes, and reports wages on a Form W-2. The employee's tax is largely handled before the money arrives. For a contractor, none of that happens: the full payment arrives untaxed, and the contractor is responsible for reporting the income, paying income tax on it, and paying the entire Social Security and Medicare amount themselves, which is the self-employment tax covered later. The contractor also gets no employer benefits, no unemployment cover, and no workplace protections that attach to employment.

## Misclassification risk

Because employees cost a business more, there is a temptation to label workers as contractors when the relationship is really employment. Authorities take this seriously, and misclassification can lead to back taxes, penalties, and liability for the payer. As the worker, being misclassified as a contractor means carrying tax and costs that should have been the employer's, so it is worth understanding which you actually are rather than accepting a label.

## You can be both

It is common to be an employee at one job and a contractor on the side, and the two are taxed separately according to their own rules. Side income paid without withholding is contractor income even if your main job is employment, and it brings the contractor obligations with it: reporting the income, paying tax on it, and likely making estimated payments if the amounts are meaningful. The day job's withholding does not cover the side income's tax.

## The practical takeaway

If you are paid without tax withheld and you control how you do the work, you are almost certainly operating as a contractor for that income, with all the responsibility that implies. The rest of this reference assumes that position. The first mental shift is that no one is setting aside your tax for you, so the structure to do it yourself is not optional; it is the core of operating as a contractor without nasty surprises.
