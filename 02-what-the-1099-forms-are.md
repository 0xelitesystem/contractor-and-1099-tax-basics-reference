# What the 1099 forms are

A 1099 is an information form that reports income paid to someone who is not an employee. The payer sends it to you and files a copy with the tax authority, so the authority already knows about income reported this way. For a contractor, the 1099 is the paperwork side of being paid without withholding, and understanding which forms apply keeps you from being caught out.

## The common forms

The form most contractors see is the one reporting payments for services, used when a business pays a non-employee 600 or more during the year for work. Payments processed through certain third-party platforms and payment apps are reported on a different form by the processor instead, and the reporting threshold for those has been changing, so it is worth confirming the current rule each year. Interest, dividends, and other kinds of income have their own 1099 variants, but for someone selling services the services form and the payment-processor form are the two that matter most.

## The threshold is about reporting, not about owing

A frequent misunderstanding is that income under the reporting threshold is not taxable. It is. The 600 figure is the point at which a payer must issue the form, not the point at which income becomes taxable. You owe tax on your business income regardless of whether any 1099 was issued, including cash, small jobs, and anything under the threshold. The form is the authority's copy; the obligation is yours either way.

## Reconcile the forms against your own records

You should keep your own record of everything you earned, and then check the 1099s you receive against it. Forms can be wrong: a client might report the wrong amount, double-count, or include a reimbursement as income. Because the authority receives the same form, a mismatch between what was reported and what you report can trigger questions. If a 1099 is incorrect, the right move is to ask the payer to correct it rather than quietly reporting a different number. Your own clean records are what let you catch the error in the first place.

## Forms you may have to issue

If your own business pays a contractor 600 or more for services in a year, you may be the one who has to issue a 1099 to them and file it. Collecting a payee's tax details up front, before you pay them, on the appropriate form, makes this straightforward at year end. Leaving it until January, when you have to chase people for tax numbers, is a predictable headache. As you grow and start paying others, you move from only receiving these forms to issuing them too.

## Keep them, but do not rely on them to tell you what you earned

Treat incoming 1099s as confirmations to reconcile, not as your bookkeeping. The contractors who struggle at tax time are usually the ones who waited for the forms to remind them what they made. The ones who find it routine tracked their income as it came in all year, and used the forms only to check that the reported totals matched what they already knew.
