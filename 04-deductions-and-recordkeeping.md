# Deductions and recordkeeping

A contractor is taxed on profit, not on revenue, which means legitimate business expenses reduce the income that tax is calculated on. The tax system allows you to deduct the ordinary and necessary costs of running your business. Claiming them correctly lowers your bill; claiming them sloppily or aggressively invites trouble. Both depend entirely on records.

## What ordinary and necessary means

The standard for a deductible business expense is that it is both ordinary, common and accepted in your line of work, and necessary, helpful and appropriate for the business. A laptop for a developer, software subscriptions, a portion of phone and internet used for work, professional services, business travel, and supplies typically qualify. Personal expenses do not, and the line between the two is exactly where care is needed. An expense used partly for business and partly personally can usually only be deducted for the business portion, which has to be reasonable and supportable.

## The records are the deduction

A deduction you cannot support is a deduction you may lose if questioned, so in practice the record is the deduction. For each business expense, keep what was bought, when, how much, and the business reason. Receipts, invoices, and bank or card statements are the evidence. The reliable approach is to capture this as you go rather than reconstruct it at year end, because reconstruction is slow, error-prone, and unconvincing if anyone asks.

## Separate business and personal money

The most useful single habit is a dedicated business bank account and, ideally, a business card, with all business income and expenses flowing through them and nothing personal mixed in. This does several things at once: it makes the records mostly automatic, it makes the business and personal pictures each readable, and it makes a tax professional's job faster and cheaper. Mixing everything in one personal account is the source of most contractor bookkeeping pain and most lost deductions, because untangling it later is tedious and some costs simply get forgotten.

## Particular areas to handle carefully

Some deductions draw more scrutiny because they are commonly misused. A home office deduction has specific rules about exclusive business use of the space. Vehicle and travel deductions require records of business versus personal use. Meals have their own partial rules. Anything that blends personal enjoyment with a business purpose deserves extra documentation. None of these are off limits when genuine; they simply need cleaner records than a plain software subscription does, because they are the ones most likely to be questioned.

## Keep records longer than you think

Tax authorities can review past years for a period after filing, so records need to survive well beyond the year they relate to. Keep the supporting documents, the bank records, and copies of what you filed for the retention period the authority specifies. Digital copies, organized by year, are enough and are far easier to keep than paper. The contractor who can answer a question about a three-year-old expense in minutes is the one who filed the receipt when it happened and never had to go looking.
