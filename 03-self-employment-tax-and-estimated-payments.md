# Self-employment tax and estimated payments

Two things surprise new contractors most: a tax that employees never see in full, and the requirement to pay tax through the year rather than once at filing. Both follow from there being no employer handling withholding. Understanding them is what turns the tax bill from a shock into a planned cost.

## The self-employment tax

Social Security and Medicare are funded by a tax split between employer and employee for those who are employed. A contractor is both the worker and the business, so they pay both halves, which together make up the self-employment tax. This is on top of ordinary income tax, and it is the part that most surprises people moving from employment, because as an employee they only ever saw their half on a payslip and the employer quietly paid the rest. The tax is calculated on your net self-employment earnings, your business income after deductible expenses, not on gross revenue. A portion of the self-employment tax is itself deductible against income tax, which softens the combined effect somewhat.

## Income tax still applies on top

The self-employment tax is separate from income tax. Your business profit also flows into your overall income and is taxed at your ordinary income tax rates, alongside any other income you have. So contractor profit can carry two layers: income tax at your rate, plus self-employment tax. This is why the fraction of contractor income that ends up owed in tax is often higher than employees expect, and why setting aside a too-small percentage is a common early mistake.

## Why estimated payments exist

The system expects tax to be paid roughly as income is earned. Employees achieve this through withholding on every paycheck. Contractors, with no withholding, are generally expected to make estimated tax payments through the year, commonly on a quarterly schedule, covering both income tax and self-employment tax. Skipping them and paying everything at filing can trigger an underpayment penalty, even if you pay the full amount eventually, because the issue is timing, not just total.

## How much and when

The payments are estimates, based on your expected income and tax for the year, and there are safe-harbor rules that can protect you from penalties if you pay at least a certain amount relative to last year's tax or this year's. The exact thresholds and due dates are set by the authority and shift, so confirm the current figures rather than relying on a remembered number. The practical point is that there is a schedule, there are amounts, and missing them costs you, so it belongs on a calendar.

## Build the habit early

The contractors who handle this smoothly treat estimated payments as a routine part of the quarter, funded from money already set aside, rather than as a scramble to find cash four times a year. The next pages cover the set-aside system that makes the payments painless. The mental model to adopt now is that a meaningful share of every payment you receive is not yours; it is tax you are holding until its due date, the same as an employer would have withheld it.
