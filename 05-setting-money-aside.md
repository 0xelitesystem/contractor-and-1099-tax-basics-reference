# Setting money aside

The defining difference between employment and contracting, from a cash point of view, is that no one withholds your tax for you. The whole payment lands in your account, and some of it is not yours. A simple set-aside system replaces the withholding an employer would have done, so the tax bill and the estimated payments are always already funded.

## Treat tax money as not yours

The mental shift that makes everything else work is to stop seeing a payment as income you can spend. Part of every payment is tax you are holding until its due date. An employee never sees that part; it is gone before the paycheck arrives. As a contractor you do see it, which is the trap, because money in your account feels spendable. Naming the tax portion as belonging to the authority, not to you, is what stops it from being spent and then missed when the bill comes.

## A separate account and a fixed percentage

The practical system is a separate savings account for tax, and a rule: every time a client pays you, move a fixed percentage into that account immediately. The percentage should reflect both your income tax and your self-employment tax, which together are often higher than people first guess, so a conservative set-aside is safer than a thin one. When estimated payments come due, you pay them from this account, which has been filling all along. At filing, any balance owed comes from the same place, and any surplus is genuinely yours.

## Size the percentage conservatively

It is far better to set aside slightly too much than too little. Setting aside too much means a pleasant surplus at year end; setting aside too little means finding cash you do not have. Until you know your real effective rate from a full year, lean high. A tax professional can help you estimate a sensible percentage for your income level and state, and you can adjust it as you learn what your actual bill looks like. The cost of over-saving is only that the money sat idle for a few months; the cost of under-saving is a scramble and possibly a penalty.

## Fund estimated payments from the same pool

Because the set-aside account is filling continuously, the quarterly estimated payments stop being a search for cash and become a transfer from money already reserved. This is the entire point: the discomfort of estimated payments comes almost entirely from not having set the money aside, not from the payments themselves. An operator who reserves as they earn finds the quarterly dates routine, while one who spends everything and then faces the payment finds them stressful, even though they owe the same amount.

## Layer it with the other buffers

Tax set-aside is one of several pools a contractor holds, alongside an operating buffer and a personal emergency fund covered elsewhere in this portfolio. Keeping them distinct matters, because tax money is the most clearly not yours of all of them, and spending it is borrowing from an obligation with a fixed due date. The operator who keeps tax separate, sets aside conservatively as money arrives, and pays estimates from that pool turns the most stressful part of contracting into a non-event. The bill is never a surprise, because it was always already saved for.
